Cash flow vs profit: why profitable shops still run out of money

The difference between profit and cash, the cash conversion cycle explained with a shop example, and practical ways to free up cash without cutting sales.

Updated 12 November 20263 min readBy the BahiKhata editorial team

A business can show a healthy profit and still be unable to pay its supplier on Friday. It happens every day, and it is almost always for the same few reasons. Understanding them is the difference between growing and struggling.

Profit is an opinion, cash is a fact

  • Profit counts a sale when it happens and an expense when it is incurred, whether or not money has moved.
  • Cash flow counts only money that has actually come in or gone out.

The gap between the two is where businesses get into trouble.

Five things that make profit and cash differ

  1. Udhaar to customers. A ₹50,000 credit sale adds to profit today but adds nothing to your bank until the customer pays.
  2. Stock on the shelf. Buying ₹1 lakh of stock uses ₹1 lakh of cash immediately, but it only becomes an expense as it is sold.
  3. Loan repayments. The principal portion of an EMI uses cash but is not an expense. Only the interest reduces profit.
  4. Equipment purchases. A ₹60,000 billing machine uses all that cash now, but only a slice of it is charged as depreciation each year.
  5. Owner's drawings and taxes. Money taken home and advance tax paid both reduce cash but not profit.

The cash conversion cycle

The cash conversion cycle is how many days your money is tied up between paying a supplier and collecting from a customer.

Cash cycle = Stock days + Debtor days − Creditor days

An example for a wholesale shop:

Measure Days
Stock sits on shelf for 40
Customers take to pay 35
Suppliers allow you 30
Cash cycle 45

For 45 days, the shop is funding its customers and its shelves from its own pocket. If monthly purchases are ₹6 lakh, that is roughly ₹9 lakh of working capital locked up. Grow sales by half and you need about ₹4.5 lakh more cash, even though profit also grows. This is why fast-growing businesses often feel the cash squeeze most.

Seven ways to free up cash

  1. Collect udhaar faster. Even cutting debtor days from 35 to 25 releases a third of the money tied up with customers. Weekly reminders from your digital khata are the cheapest lever you have.
  2. Offer a small discount for immediate payment. 1% for payment within 7 days often costs less than the interest on a working-capital loan.
  3. Buy little and often. Smaller, more frequent orders reduce stock days, especially for slow movers.
  4. Clear dead stock. Items that have not sold in six months are cash on a shelf. Discount them and reinvest.
  5. Negotiate supplier terms. Moving from 30 to 45 days of supplier credit cuts your cycle by 15 days.
  6. Match loan type to need. Use a working-capital limit for stock and udhaar, and a term loan for equipment. Check the EMI with our EMI calculator before you sign.
  7. Forecast the next 8 weeks. List expected receipts and payments week by week. Gaps show up while there is still time to act.

A simple weekly cash forecast

Week Opening cash Expected in Expected out Closing cash
1 ₹80,000 ₹1,40,000 ₹1,65,000 ₹55,000
2 ₹55,000 ₹1,20,000 ₹1,95,000 −₹20,000

Week 2 shows a shortfall a full week ahead, while you can still chase customers or delay a purchase.

Common questions

Is cash flow more important than profit?

In the short term, yes: a business with no cash cannot pay bills even if it is profitable on paper. In the long term you need both, because a business that never makes a profit eventually runs out of cash too.

What is a good cash conversion cycle?

It depends on the trade. Grocery retail can run near zero or even negative, while distributors and manufacturers often run 30 to 90 days. The trend matters more than the number: if it is getting longer, act.

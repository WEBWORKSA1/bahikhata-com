Walk into an old wholesale market in Ahmedabad, Jodhpur or Kolkata and you will still see it on a counter: a tall, narrow book bound in red cloth, tied with a cotton string, its pages folded lengthwise and filled with neat columns of figures. That is a **bahikhata** (बही खाता), the account book that Indian traders have used for centuries.

The words are simple. *Bahi* means a bound book or register. *Khata* means an account. Put together, a bahikhata is the book in which a business keeps its accounts with every person it deals with.

## Why the book is red

The red cloth cover is not decoration. Red is considered auspicious, and many families open a fresh bahi on an auspicious day, most often around Diwali, with a small prayer on the first page. In Gujarat the ritual is called *chopda pujan*; in many Marwari and North Indian families it is part of the Diwali Lakshmi puja. The cloth binding also had a practical job: it survived damp godowns and years of handling far better than paper covers.

The tradition became national news in July 2019, when the Finance Minister carried the Union Budget papers in a red bahikhata instead of the leather briefcase used since colonial times.

## The books inside a traditional bahi system

A trader rarely kept just one book. The system usually had at least two:

| Book | What it records | Modern equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| Rokad bahi (cash book) | Every rupee that came in or went out, day by day | Cash book |
| Khata bahi (ledger) | One page or section per person, showing what they owe or are owed | Party ledger, accounts receivable and payable |
| Nakal bahi or kachchi bahi | Rough daily notes before they were written neatly | Day book or journal |
| Hundi register | Bills of exchange sent and received | Bills receivable and payable |

Entries were first written in the rokad bahi as they happened. Later, often at the end of the day, the *munim* (the accountant) posted each entry into the right person's khata. That two-step flow is the heart of the system.

## Naam and jama: the two sides

Every khata page is split into two sides:

- **Naam** (नाम), also called *udhaar* in everyday speech, is the debit side. When you give goods or money to someone on credit, you write it in their naam. They now owe you.
- **Jama** (जमा) is the credit side. When that person pays you, or when you receive goods from them on credit, it goes in jama.

The difference between the two sides is the *baaki* (balance). If naam is bigger, the person owes you. If jama is bigger, you owe them.

## Single entry or double entry?

School textbooks often describe bahikhata as a single-entry system. Several accounting historians disagree. They point out that a transaction was recorded once in the cash book and again in a person's account, so cash and personal accounts always moved together. That is a genuine double entry for real and personal accounts. What the traditional system usually did not do was keep formal nominal accounts for every expense and income head, or prepare a balance sheet in the modern format.

The practical lesson is the same either way: **record every transaction in two places, and the two places must agree**. If the cash book says ₹5,000 came in from Sharma ji, Sharma ji's khata must show ₹5,000 in jama.

## Why traders trusted it

A well-kept bahi did three things that every business still needs:

1. **It created a written record of trust.** Credit in Indian markets has always run on relationships. The bahi turned a handshake into a figure both sides could check.
2. **It was evidence.** Regularly kept account books have long been accepted by Indian courts as relevant evidence of a transaction. Section 34 of the old Evidence Act dealt with them, and the rule continues in the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023. Courts still expect other proof alongside the entries, but a neat, regular bahi carries weight.
3. **It showed the health of the business at a glance.** Flip to the back pages and a seth could see how much was stuck with customers and how much he owed suppliers.

## What the paper book could not do

The red book has real limits in a busy modern shop:

- Totals are calculated by hand, so one mistake carries forward for months.
- There is no backup. Fire, water or a lost book wipes out years of records.
- Customers cannot see their own statement without visiting the shop.
- Reminders depend on memory and awkward conversations.
- GST invoices, bank payments and UPI receipts live in separate places.

That is why most shops now keep a digital khata alongside, or instead of, the paper book. The method does not change. Naam is still naam and jama is still jama. Only the arithmetic, the backup and the reminders move to the phone.

## Bringing the habit into a digital khata

If you keep a paper bahi today, you do not need to throw it away. A sensible way to switch:

1. Pick a clean starting date, such as the first day of a month.
2. Write down each customer's and supplier's closing baaki from the paper book on that date.
3. Enter those as opening balances in a [digital khata]({{root}}khata/).
4. From the starting date onward, record every new entry digitally.
5. Keep the paper book as your archive for earlier years.
